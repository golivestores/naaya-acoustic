Approach — Room Acoustics

Reverberation time
& the Sabine limit

The reverberation-time formula every acoustician starts with — where it holds, where it quietly breaks, and what to reach for when a room refuses to behave.

Scroll
Abstract

Reverberation time is the first number most acousticians reach for, and the Sabine equation is how they get it. This note revisits that equation as a working tool — the assumptions baked into it, the room conditions where it stays honest, and the common cases where it drifts far enough to mislead. We close with the practical corrections and on-site checks NAAYA uses on real projects.

TopicRoom Acoustics
Published2026
AuthorNAAYA Acoustics Team
Reading time8 minutes
01 — The equation

What Sabine actually says

In 1898 Wallace Clement Sabine reduced the decay of sound in a room to a single, elegant relationship: reverberation time grows with the volume of the space and falls as you add absorption. In modern form, RT₃₀ = 0.161 V / A, where V is room volume in cubic metres and A is the total absorption in sabins — the sum of every surface area multiplied by its absorption coefficient.

The equation’s appeal is that it needs almost nothing: a volume, a handful of materials, and their coefficients. For a first pass on a rectangular room with evenly spread absorption it is remarkably close to reality — which is exactly why it has survived for more than a century.

02 — Where it holds

Diffuse fields and even absorption

Sabine’s derivation assumes a diffuse sound field — energy arriving equally from all directions, with absorption spread evenly around the room. Under those conditions every sound ray sees the same average absorption on each reflection, and the decay is a clean exponential.

Most ordinary rooms — classrooms, offices, rehearsal spaces, moderately furnished halls — are close enough to this ideal that the formula predicts RT within a comfortable tolerance. It is the honest workhorse of everyday design.

Sabine is not wrong so much as trusting: it believes every reflection sees the same room.
03 — Where it breaks

Three rooms that misbehave

The trouble starts when the field stops being diffuse. Highly absorptive rooms — recording studios, anechoic-leaning spaces — violate Sabine’s assumption that each ray survives many reflections; here the Eyring formulation is closer. Uneven absorption — a dead ceiling over hard parallel walls — produces flutter and a decay that no single number describes well. And coupled volumes — a stage house joined to an auditorium — decay in two stages the simple model cannot see at all.

04 — In practice

How we use it, and what we check

On a live project, Sabine is the start of the conversation, not the end. We use it to size absorption quickly and to sanity-check a target, then switch to Eyring for absorptive rooms and to ray-tracing simulation whenever the geometry is complex, the absorption is uneven, or volumes are coupled.

Finally, we verify on site. A measured RT₃₀ against the predicted value tells us whether the room really is behaving as the model assumed — and when it isn’t, the gap itself is diagnostic. That closing loop, from formula to simulation to measurement, is what keeps a prediction honest.
